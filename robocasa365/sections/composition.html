§4.4 · What pretraining data actually matters
pp. 14–15

Pretraining pays off — but which pretraining? Holding the recipe fixed (GR00T N1.5, pretrain then post-train) and swapping only the pretraining mixture isolates the one variable that ships with every foundation model: the data it was fed.

Four mixtures compete, from nothing to the full firehose: No Pretraining, Human50 (human demos from 50 tasks — the Atomic and Composite-Seen tasks plus a random handful more), Human300 (human demos from all 300 tasks), and Human300 + MG60 (adds MimicGen-generated synthetic trajectories across 60 tasks).

Each mixture is scored twice: a low-data regime with 10% of the target data and a high-data regime with the full 100%, across the Atomic, Composite-Seen, and Composite-Unseen splits.

Reading the two Average rows side by side surfaces the two findings that matter — and one of them cuts against intuition.

First, task diversity wins: Human300 beats Human50 everywhere, and the gap is widest in the data-starved 10% regime (40.0 vs 34.7). The single largest jump lands on Composite-Unseen (23.8 → 32.3) — broadening the range of pretraining tasks is precisely what helps a policy reach for goals it never trained on.

Second, and counterintuitively, synthetic data hurts: adding MimicGen's 60 tasks drops the average below plain Human300 in both regimes (35.9 vs 40.0 at 10%, 51.1 vs 52.5 at 100%). MimicGen can generate demonstrations at massive scale, but they vary in quality — and here quantity of mixed-quality data loses to a smaller pool of clean human demos.

Takeaway
More tasks in pretraining help most — especially for novel goals in the low-data regime — but more data only helps if it is clean: bolting on large-scale synthetic demos of uneven quality drags downstream success below human-only pretraining, leaving how to learn from big mixed-quality datasets as open work.
