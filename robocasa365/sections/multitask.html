§4.1 · Multi-task training at scale
pp. 6–7

The first stress test is the hardest thing a generalist should be able to do: train one language-conditioned, vision-based policy on all 300 pretraining tasks at once — 100 human demonstrations each, 30k in total — and see how far today's best methods get.

Four state-of-the-art recipes go head to head: Diffusion Policy, and three vision-language-action models — π0, π0.5, and GR00T N1.5 — the latter three fine-tuned from public pretrained checkpoints.

Evaluated across these 50 tasks in the pretraining kitchens, the percentage of successful task completions falls in a clean gradient.

Difficulty ranks exactly as intuition predicts — Atomic (easiest) < Composite-Seen < Composite-Unseen — because longer horizons give errors more room to compound, and the unseen split was never trained on at all.

Grouping the same numbers by split makes the method ordering pop: GR00T N1.5 sits on top of every bar, and the whole VLA trio towers over Diffusion Policy.

GR00T N1.5 leads every split and overall (average 20.0), trailed by π0.5, then π0, with Diffusion Policy last at 6.1 — evidence that high-capacity vision-language-action models fit large, diverse multi-task robot data far better than a smaller policy can.

The nonzero Composite-Unseen scores — GR00T's 4.4 chief among them — are the encouraging signal: these policies transfer something to tasks they were never trained on. But the win comes with an explicit caveat: batch-size compute, data composition, and whether the visual and language backbones are fine-tuned all move these numbers, so GR00T is not declared conclusively best.

Takeaway
Big VLA models beat Diffusion Policy across the board and even score above zero on zero-shot composite tasks — but with the best method under 10% on anything longer than an atomic skill, the headroom on multi-task manipulation is enormous.
