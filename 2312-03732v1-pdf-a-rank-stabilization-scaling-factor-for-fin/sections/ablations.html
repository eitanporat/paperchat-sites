Appendix B · Ablations
pp. 9–12

The central claim — that gradient collapse under LoRA is caused by the scaling factor, not by the optimizer, model family, dataset, or adapter placement — is stress-tested across six controlled probes. Each ablation isolates one degree of freedom while holding the rest fixed, collectively ruling out the most natural competing explanations for the observed collapse.

B.1 — SGD rules out optimizer-side stabilization

AdamW's adaptive per-parameter learning rates could, in principle, be compensating for collapsing gradients by rescaling updates — masking the underlying instability. Replacing AdamW with vanilla SGD at a fixed learning rate of 1 × 10⁻⁴ removes this confound entirely. The gradient-norm pattern is identical to the AdamW run: LoRA collapses with rank while rsLoRA keeps all ranks within the same order of magnitude. In fact, rsLoRA with SGD is more stable — all ranks hold nearly the same norm value for roughly the first 100 training steps.

B.2 — Different model, optimizer, and dataset

Switching from Llama 2 7B + AdamW + OpenOrca to GPT-J 6B + Adafactor + GSM8k (grade-school math word problems) produces the same qualitative outcome. All LoRA training curves collapse so completely that they overlap into a single band — indistinguishable by rank. rsLoRA curves separate cleanly, with higher ranks reaching lower perplexity.

B.3 — Scaled initialization alone is not enough

One might ask whether the fix belongs in the initialization of A rather than in γ_r. Two probes answer this. First: drop γ_r entirely and scale A's initialization by 1/√r — gradient norms start equal at ranks 4 and 2048, but training becomes numerically unstable for rank 2048 mid-run. Second: use the scaled initialization and LoRA's 1/r scaling factor — the result is identically sub-optimal to plain LoRA. The corrective factor must live in γ_r throughout training, not just at initialization.

B.4 — LR sweep over LoRA-rank-4 cannot recover capacity

A skeptic could argue that rsLoRA's larger γ_r at high rank simply acts as an effective learning-rate boost. To test this, LoRA at rank 4 is swept over a broad grid of learning rates — {5, 1} × 10n for n ∈ {−4, −3, −2} — more than two orders of magnitude. The best result anywhere on that grid still loses to rsLoRA at rank 2048 using the untuned default learning rate.

The gap confirms that the performance difference arises from genuine rank capacity: higher-rank adapters span a richer subspace of weight updates, and that capacity is only unlocked when γ_r ∝ 1/√r prevents the gradients from collapsing into it.

B.5 — Attention-only adapter placement

Standard LoRA practice often restricts adapters to the attention projection matrices, omitting the MLP feed-forward layers. This placement preserves the collapse pattern in both directions: LoRA gradient norms still fan out across ranks, and rsLoRA norms still cluster. Higher rsLoRA ranks still converge to lower perplexity; higher LoRA ranks still offer no benefit. The phenomenon is not a side-effect of adapter placement.

B.6 — Other exponents bracket the √r choice

With all six ranks collapsed onto a single comparison at r = 2048, four scaling exponents are evaluated: γ_r = 1/r² (more aggressive than LoRA), 1/r (LoRA), 1/r^{1/4} (less aggressive than LoRA), and 1/√r (rsLoRA). The outcomes align precisely with the theorem's prediction. Reducing the exponent below 1/2 — i.e., dividing by less than √r — produces unstable or diverging training. Increasing it above 1/2 — dividing by more than √r — produces the familiar LoRA-style collapse. Only γ_r ∝ 1/√r threads the needle between instability and collapse.

Takeaway
Across six controlled ablations — varying optimizer (SGD, Adafactor), model (GPT-J 6B), dataset (GSM8k), adapter placement (attention-only), initialization scheme, and scaling-factor exponent — the conclusion is unchanged: rank-stabilized gradients require exactly γ_r ∝ 1/√r, and no combination of learning-rate tuning or initialization adjustments can substitute for the correct exponent.
