Fine-tuning a large language model on a downstream task means updating hundreds of millions — often billions — of parameters, an expense that grows with model scale and quickly exhausts the memory budgets of most practitioners. Parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT) sidesteps this by optimising a small auxiliary structure while the pretrained weights stay frozen.

Low-Rank Adapters (LoRA) is among the most widely adopted PEFT approaches. Each linear layer with weight matrix $W \in \mathbb{R}^{d_2 \times d_1}$ is augmented by a trainable rank-$r$ correction: the forward pass becomes $x_\text{out} = (W + \gamma_r BA)\,x_\text{in}$, where $A \in \mathbb{R}^{r \times d_1}$ and $B \in \mathbb{R}^{d_2 \times r}$ are the adapter parameters and $\gamma_r$ is a rank-dependent scaling factor. After fine-tuning, the adapter is folded directly into $W$, so inference sees a single matrix and pays no extra cost regardless of rank.

The core tension
Choosing a higher rank $r$ increases the adapter's representational capacity — but only if the optimizer can actually learn through it. The scaling factor $\gamma_r$ determines whether that capacity is reachable.

The conventional choice sets $\gamma_r = \alpha / r$ for a fixed hyperparameter $\alpha$. Dividing by $r$ is too aggressive. As $r$ grows, the scaled product $\gamma_r BA$ shrinks toward zero and its gradient — which scales as $\Theta_r(\gamma_r^2 r)$ — collapses. The result is that training with rank 128 converges to nearly the same loss as rank 4: the extra parameters exist but cannot be used effectively.

The correct scaling follows from requiring the adapter outputs and their gradients to remain $\Theta_r(1)$ — bounded away from zero and infinity — as $r \to \infty$. That condition is satisfied if and only if $\gamma_r \in \Theta_r(1/\sqrt{r})$, i.e. dividing by $\sqrt{r}$ instead of $r$. Setting $\gamma_r = \alpha / \sqrt{r}$ defines rsLoRA.

The practical upshot is a clean compute-performance trade-off. Because the adapter is folded back into $W$ at the end of training, deployment cost is rank-independent. A practitioner can therefore spend more training compute by increasing $r$ and receive strictly better fine-tuning performance — something that the standard $1/r$ factor makes impossible.
