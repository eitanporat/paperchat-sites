§1 · Introduction
pp. 1–2

Representation learning has split into two distinct traditions. Reconstruction-based methods — today's large language models — train by predicting the exact next token, operating entirely in input space. Joint Embedding Predictive Architectures (JEPAs) take a different path: instead of reconstructing raw input, they enforce that different views of the same underlying concept can be predicted from each other in embedding space. In vision, this distinction matters enormously — JEPAs provably avoid the noisy low-level feature bias that haunts pixel-reconstruction objectives , at the cost of some risk of dimensional collapse . In language, the JEPA side of the ledger has been blank.

The obstacle is finding views. Vision datasets generate views for free — two photos of the same building at different times of day, two crops of the same image. Language datasets already contain views too, hiding in plain sight: a natural-language description and the regex it encodes, a SQL question and the query that answers it, a GitHub issue and the code diff that resolves it . Each pair encodes the same underlying knowledge in two different surface forms — exactly the structure JEPA requires.

LLM-JEPA exploits this structure by adding one embedding-space term to the standard next-token loss — nothing else about the model or its generative objective changes. During training, the encoder embeds Text and Code independently (via a block-causal attention mask), a lightweight tied-weight predictor maps Enc(Text) toward Enc(Code), and cosine distance between the prediction and the target embedding forms the JEPA penalty. The generative loss runs unchanged alongside it.

Core contribution
Treating (text, code) pairs as two views of one underlying knowledge unlocks JEPA objectives for LLMs — a single embedding-space prediction term added to the generative loss, with no change to the model architecture and no sacrifice of generative capability.

The headline result: across four model families (Llama-3.2, Gemma-2, OpenELM, OLMo) and four datasets (NL-RX-SYNTH, NL-RX-TURK, Spider, GSM8K), LLM-JEPA consistently outperforms next-token-prediction finetuning. On NL-RX-SYNTH with Llama-3.2-1B, accuracy jumps from 51.6 % to 66.6 %. The gain compounds across epochs: where standard finetuning overfits after a few epochs, LLM-JEPA keeps improving — the two training curves are the clearest signature of what the embedding-space term is adding.

The epoch-by-epoch trajectory (Figure 1b in the paper) reinforces the point: over six training epochs on NL-RX-SYNTH and NL-RX-TURK, the baseline peaks early and degrades, while LLM-JEPA reaches 70.4 → 70.9 → 71.8 % on SYNTH across the final epochs. Overfitting resistance is not a side effect — it is a direct consequence of the JEPA term regularizing the representation space, as the structured embedding analysis in §4.2 confirms.
