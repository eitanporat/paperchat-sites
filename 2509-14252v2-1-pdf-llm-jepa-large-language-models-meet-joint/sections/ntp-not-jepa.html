§3.3 · Diagnostic
pp. 4–5

Before scaling to many models and datasets, one sanity check is essential: does standard next-token prediction already minimise the JEPA alignment term as a side effect? If it did, adding an explicit JEPA loss would be redundant. The answer, tested on Llama-3.2-1B-Instruct with NL-RX-SYNTH, is a clear no.

Two controlled runs share identical architecture and data. The first trains with the standard cross-entropy loss L_LLM while monitoring — but not back-propagating — the JEPA prediction error d(Pred(Enc(Text)), Enc(Code)). That monitored signal stays flat: NTP optimisation never pressures the encoder to align Text and Code embeddings. The second run adds the JEPA gradient. Now d(·,·) falls steadily — and the cross-entropy curve of the two runs is indistinguishable. The NTP objective is completely unaffected by the extra term.

The singular-value spectrum makes the geometry legible. For the base model and the NTP fine-tune, the top singular values of Enc(Text) − Enc(Code) remain in the hundreds — the mapping from Text to Code embeddings sprawls across a high-dimensional subspace. Under LLM-JEPA the same spectrum drops one to two orders of magnitude: the mapping is confined to a narrow subspace, which is precisely the structure the JEPA objective imposes. This mirrors what was observed for vision autoencoders — reconstruction-based training leaves perceptual structure underspecified, while embedding-space objectives compress it into something geometrically tight.

The accuracy gap makes the downstream consequence concrete: 51.95% for NTP versus 71.10% for LLM-JEPA on NL-RX-SYNTH — a gain of nearly twenty points purchased entirely in the geometry of the latent space, not in any change to next-token behaviour.

Takeaway
NTP and JEPA optimise orthogonal objectives: NTP never shrinks the embedding-alignment gap on its own, yet adding the JEPA term leaves the NTP loss curve unchanged. Every accuracy gain comes from latent-space geometry, not tokenwise prediction.
