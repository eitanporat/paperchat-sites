§2 · Background
pp. 2–3

Every modern LLM is trained to predict the next token. Given a sequence packed into a single context window, the model reads tokens Text_{1:L-1} through an encoder and a linear classifier head, then minimises the cross-entropy against the true next token Text_L. Summed autoregressively over all positions, this one objective has driven GPT, PaLM, and their descendants.

This input-space reconstruction objective is deliberately preserved in LLM-JEPA — it is not replaced but augmented. That design choice is what separates this work from every prior attempt to bring embedding-space training to language.

Earlier approaches discovered that the embedding space is a richer place to supervise language representations than the token surface, but each paid a steep price. SimCSE treats two dropout-perturbed views of the same sentence as positives in a contrastive loss, producing state-of-the-art sentence embeddings — but the resulting model cannot generate text. Sentence-T5 scales encoder representations from pretrained T5 checkpoints with no generative output. Weakly supervised text-pair contrastive training learns joint embeddings from naturally paired sentences, again forfeiting the decoder. Sentence-BERT grafts a semantic similarity loss onto BERT, improving performance on semantic tasks — but BERT has no autoregressive generation to protect in the first place.

The trade-off every prior method accepted
Embedding-space objectives improve representation quality for retrieval and classification, but the models they produce cannot generate — making them incompatible with the evaluation protocols and downstream use-cases that define modern LLM utility.

The pattern is consistent: move supervision into embedding space, lose the generator. LLM-JEPA breaks that trade-off by treating L_LLM as a floor, not a ceiling — and then stacking a JEPA objective on top of it without touching the generative pathway at all.
